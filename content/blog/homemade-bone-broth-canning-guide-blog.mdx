Homemade bone broth has become one of the most practical kitchen projects for anyone serious about whole-food cooking. A single large batch made from roasted bones costs a fraction of what premium store-bought broth sells for, and it produces a collagen-rich, deeply flavored liquid that commercial broths — even the expensive ones — rarely match.

The challenge is what to do with a large batch. Bone broth keeps in the refrigerator for only 5–7 days, and it takes 6–24 hours of simmering to make. If you are going to spend that much time on it, you need a preservation method that makes the effort worthwhile. This is where proper canning comes in — and where home cooks most often make a dangerous mistake.

---

## The Most Important Thing to Know: Water Bath Canning Will Not Work

Before anything else, this needs to be stated clearly: **bone broth cannot be safely preserved through water bath canning.** Water bath canning only reaches 212°F (100°C), the boiling point of water. Bone broth is a low-acid food. At 212°F, *Clostridium botulinum* spores — which cause botulism — are not reliably destroyed in low-acid foods. Botulism is tasteless, odorless, and potentially fatal.

The only safe method for canning bone broth at home is **pressure canning**, which raises internal temperature to 240°F (116°C) under pressure, sufficient to destroy botulinum spores in low-acid foods. If you do not own a pressure canner, your two safe options are refrigeration (up to 7 days) or freezing (up to 6 months).

This is not a technicality — it is a food safety requirement that applies to every home-canned meat broth.

## Making the Bone Broth

**Best bones to use:**
- Beef: knuckle bones, neck bones, oxtail, marrow bones, short rib bones — any combination
- Chicken: carcasses, backs, feet (chicken feet add significant collagen), necks
- Mixed: combining beef and chicken bones is common and produces a rich, complex broth

**Roasting the bones first (strongly recommended):**
Spread bones on a rimmed baking sheet and roast at 425°F for 30–45 minutes until deeply browned. This Maillard reaction adds enormous depth of flavor — a step most store-bought broths skip entirely. The browned fond (caramelized bits) on the pan should be deglazed into the pot with a splash of cold water.

**The basic method:**
1. Place roasted bones in a large stockpot or slow cooker with enough cold water to cover by 2–3 inches.
2. Add 2 tablespoons of apple cider vinegar. The mild acidity helps draw collagen and minerals from the bones into the broth. You will not taste it in the finished product.
3. Add aromatics: 2 onions (halved), 4 carrots (broken in half), 4 celery stalks, 1 head of garlic (halved crosswise), a handful of peppercorns, and bay leaves.
4. Bring to a boil, skim off any foam that rises, then reduce to a bare simmer.
5. Simmer time: **chicken broth: 6–12 hours; beef broth: 12–24 hours.** The longer the simmer, the more collagen converts to gelatin and dissolves into the liquid.
6. Strain through a fine-mesh strainer. Discard solids.
7. Refrigerate overnight. The fat will solidify on top and can be skimmed off completely.

A properly made bone broth will gel when cold — this is the collagen (gelatin) that gives it nutritional and culinary value. If your broth does not gel, the bones were likely not collagen-rich enough, or the simmer time was insufficient.

## Pressure Canning Bone Broth Safely

You will need a **USDA-approved pressure canner** (not a pressure cooker — they are different appliances with different sealing systems) and standard Mason jars with new lids.

**Processing guidelines (from the USDA):**
- Pints: **20 minutes** at 11 lbs pressure (dial gauge) or 10 lbs (weighted gauge)
- Quarts: **25 minutes** at 11 lbs pressure (dial gauge) or 10 lbs (weighted gauge)

Note: At altitudes above 1,000 feet, increase pressure according to your canner's instructions. Always follow the most current USDA guidelines for your specific canner model.

**Process:**
1. Reheat the broth to a boil before filling jars.
2. Fill hot jars, leaving 1 inch of headspace.
3. Wipe jar rims clean, apply lids and bands finger-tight.
4. Process according to your pressure canner's instructions for the correct time and pressure.
5. Allow pressure to return to zero naturally before opening the canner.
6. Let jars cool for 12–24 hours undisturbed. Check seals before storing — any lid that flexes up and down has not sealed and should be refrigerated and used within a week.

Properly sealed, pressure-canned bone broth stores in a cool, dark pantry for **1–2 years**.

## Freezing as a Practical Alternative

If you do not own a pressure canner, freezing is the simplest preservation method. Pour cooled broth into:
- **Ice cube trays** — freeze, then transfer cubes to a freezer bag. One cube ≈ 1–2 tablespoons, perfect for deglazing pans or finishing sauces.
- **Muffin tins** — approximately ½ cup per portion, useful for rice cooking or quick soup bases.
- **Freezer-safe quart bags or containers** — for full-portion use.

Bone broth keeps frozen for up to **6 months** with no quality loss.

## Why Homemade Beats Store-Bought

Most commercial bone broths are made quickly (under 4 hours) and do not gel when chilled, which means they contain little to no actual gelatin from collagen. The label "bone broth" is largely unregulated. Homemade broth that gels solid in the refrigerator contains meaningful amounts of glycine, proline, and hydroxyproline — the amino acids in collagen — as well as minerals leached from the bones during the long simmer.

The cost comparison is also significant: a batch made from saved roasted chicken carcasses (free if you save them) or beef knuckle bones (typically $2–4/lb) produces 4–6 quarts of broth for less than $5, versus $6–8 per quart for premium store-bought bone broth.
