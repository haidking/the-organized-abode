Eggs are the most versatile ingredient in any kitchen, yet they are also one of the most frequently mishandled. Most home cooks have a go-to method — usually scrambled — and rarely venture beyond it. The result is that an ingredient capable of producing seven completely different dishes becomes a daily disappointment: rubbery scrambles, gray-ringed boiled eggs, or broken yolks from a clumsy flip.

The reason most people struggle is not lack of effort — it is a misunderstanding of how egg proteins behave under heat. Once you understand the basic science, every method becomes predictable and repeatable.

---

## The Science Behind Cooking Eggs

Eggs contain two distinct proteins that set at different temperatures. **Egg whites begin to coagulate at around 144°F (62°C)**, while **yolks set between 149°F and 158°F (65–70°C)**. This temperature gap is the key to nearly every egg technique.

When you apply heat too quickly or at too high a temperature, both the whites and yolks seize simultaneously, squeezing out water and creating that familiar rubbery texture in scrambled eggs or the green-gray ring around a hard-boiled yolk. Controlling temperature — not just time — is what separates a good egg from a great one.

## 7 Classic Egg Styles: Technique Breakdown

### 1. Scrambled Eggs
The biggest mistake home cooks make with scrambled eggs is using high heat. High heat forces protein strands to contract tightly, squeezing out moisture and creating dry, grainy curds.

**The correct method:** Whisk eggs with a splash of whole milk or cream, season lightly, and pour into a non-stick pan over **medium-low heat** with a knob of butter. Use a rubber spatula to slowly fold the eggs from the edges toward the center, removing the pan from heat occasionally. The eggs should still look slightly underdone when you plate them — residual heat finishes the cooking.

**Target texture:** Large, soft, glossy curds that hold together without weeping liquid.

### 2. Sunny Side Up
Cook over medium-low heat in butter without flipping. The white should be fully set while the yolk remains runny and bright. Covering the pan with a lid for the last 30 seconds creates steam that gently sets the top of the white without overcooking the yolk — this is called the "basted" method.

### 3. Over Easy
Begin exactly like a sunny-side up egg, then flip gently once the white is set. Cook for just 20–30 seconds on the second side. The yolk should still run freely when broken. A wide, flexible spatula and butter-coated pan prevent tearing.

### 4. Soft Boiled Eggs
Bring a pot of water to a **gentle rolling boil** — not a violent boil, which knocks eggs against the pot and causes cracking. Lower eggs in gently with a spoon and cook for exactly **6 minutes and 30 seconds**. Immediately transfer to an ice-water bath for 3 minutes. The white will be fully set; the yolk will be jammy and orange-centered.

The ice bath is not optional — it halts cooking instantly and contracts the egg inside the shell, making peeling significantly easier.

### 5. Hard Boiled Eggs
Use the same method as soft boiled but cook for **10–12 minutes**. The ice bath is equally critical here: without it, residual heat continues cooking the yolk, producing the green-gray sulfur ring that forms when hydrogen sulfide reacts with iron in the yolk at elevated temperatures.

### 6. Poached Eggs
Fill a saucepan with 3 inches of water and bring to a gentle simmer (small bubbles rising slowly, not a rolling boil). Add 1 tablespoon of white vinegar — the acidity helps the egg whites firm up quickly around the yolk instead of spreading into wispy strands. Crack the egg into a small ramekin first. Create a slow whirlpool in the water with a spoon and gently slide the egg into the center. Cook for exactly **3 minutes**. Lift out with a slotted spoon and drain on a paper towel.

### 7. Fried (Crispy Edges)
For diner-style fried eggs with crispy, lacy white edges and a runny yolk, use more fat than you think you need — 2 tablespoons of butter or olive oil — in a hot pan over **medium-high heat**. The egg goes in immediately. Baste the top of the yolk with the hot fat using a spoon. Total cook time: 2–3 minutes.

## Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

**Scrambled eggs are watery:** The heat was too high, or the eggs were overcooked. Lower the heat and pull the pan off the burner periodically.

**Boiled eggs won't peel cleanly:** Skip the ice bath and the white sticks to the shell membrane. Always ice-bath for at least 3 minutes.

**Poached eggs spread everywhere:** Either the water was boiling too vigorously, or no vinegar was used. Gentle simmer + vinegar = compact poached eggs.

**Fried eggs stick to the pan:** The pan was not hot enough before the egg went in, or insufficient fat was used. Let the butter foam and subside before adding the egg.

## Storage Notes

Hard and soft boiled eggs (unpeeled) keep in the refrigerator for up to **one week**. Peeled boiled eggs should be stored in cold water in a covered container and consumed within **5 days**. Scrambled, fried, and poached eggs are best consumed immediately.
