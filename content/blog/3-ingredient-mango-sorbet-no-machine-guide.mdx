Homemade sorbet without an ice cream machine is one of those recipes that seems simple but has a significant failure mode: most attempts produce an icy, crystalline block that has to be stabbed with a fork rather than scooped. The texture is closer to a granita than a sorbet.

The reason this happens is a physical chemistry problem, and solving it requires understanding what makes ice cream and sorbet smooth. Once you know the mechanism, the fix is straightforward — and yes, it works with only 3 ingredients.

---

## Why Sorbet Goes Icy (The Science)

When you freeze fruit purée without churning, large ice crystals form. These crystals are what creates the icy, crunchy texture. Commercial ice cream and sorbet machines solve this by continuously agitating the mixture as it freezes, which physically breaks up ice crystals as they form and incorporates air, resulting in small, evenly distributed crystals and a smooth texture.

Without a machine, you need to interrupt crystallization through chemistry rather than mechanical action. There are two ways to do this:

**Sugar concentration:** Sugar lowers the freezing point of water. A high sugar-to-water ratio means the sorbet freezes at a lower temperature, giving crystals less time to grow large before the mixture solidifies. The problem with going sugar-heavy is an overly sweet result.

**Pectin and natural fruit fiber:** This is the better approach for mango. Ripe mangoes are naturally high in pectin — a carbohydrate that acts as a network-forming agent in the fruit matrix. When blended, the mango's pectin binds some of the water, limiting its availability to form large ice crystals. This is why fully ripe mango sorbet freezes with a better texture than less ripe mango sorbet — higher sugar and higher pectin.

**The no-machine technique:** The trick that solves the problem for home cooks without a machine is the **re-blend method**: freeze the sorbet base partially (until it is slushy but not solid, about 2 hours), then blend it again to break up the forming crystals, then freeze solid. This mechanically mimics what a churner does.

## The 3 Ingredients and Why Each Matters

### 1. Ripe Frozen Mango
Fresh mango works, but frozen ripe mango (available in most grocery store freezer sections year-round) is more practical and produces better results for two reasons: it is harvested and frozen at peak ripeness, so sugar and pectin content are optimal, and starting from frozen shortens the active freezing time dramatically.

If using fresh mango: peel, dice, freeze on a baking sheet for 2 hours before proceeding.

### 2. Fresh Lime Juice
The acid from lime juice does two things: it brightens and sharpens the mango flavor (without it, the sorbet can taste flat and one-dimensional despite using excellent mango), and it slightly lowers the mixture's pH, which stabilizes the beta-carotene pigments in mango and keeps the color vivid rather than dulling during freezing.

Do not substitute bottled lime juice — the volatile aromatic compounds responsible for fresh lime's bright top note are not present in bottled juice.

### 3. Honey or Agave (optional, to taste)
Ripe mango is naturally sweet enough that additional sweetener is optional. Start without it and taste the blended base — if the mango is very sweet, no sweetener is needed. If it tastes slightly tart or flat, add honey or agave in 1-teaspoon increments. Agave is slightly more neutral in flavor; honey adds its own flavor note that some people prefer and others find distracting in a fruit sorbet.

## Step-by-Step Method

1. **Blend:** Combine 3 cups frozen ripe mango chunks, 2 tablespoons fresh lime juice, and optional sweetener in a high-powered blender. Blend until completely smooth — about 90 seconds. If your blender struggles, add 2–3 tablespoons of water to help it move.

2. **First freeze:** Pour into a shallow baking dish (a 9×13 pan works well — more surface area means faster, more even freezing). Freeze for 1.5–2 hours until slushy but not fully solid. The edges will be firmer than the center.

3. **Re-blend:** Scrape the partially frozen sorbet into the blender and blend again until smooth and creamy, about 60 seconds. This breaks up the ice crystals that have formed.

4. **Final freeze:** Transfer to a freezer-safe container with a tight lid. Press plastic wrap directly onto the surface of the sorbet before sealing (this prevents freezer burn and ice crystal formation on the top layer). Freeze for at least 4 hours or overnight.

5. **Serve:** Remove from the freezer 5–10 minutes before serving to soften slightly. Scoop with a warm spoon (run under hot water briefly).

## Texture Troubleshooting

**Still icy after the re-blend:** The mango was not ripe enough (low pectin), or the re-blend step was skipped. Use riper fruit or add 1 tablespoon of vodka — alcohol does not freeze and prevents excessive hardening without affecting flavor at this small quantity.

**Too soft, won't hold a scoop:** Too much sugar, or the container is too warm. Make sure the final container goes directly into the coldest part of the freezer.

**Dull orange color:** The lime juice was insufficient, or bottled lime was used. Fresh lime juice preserves the carotenoid pigments.

## Variations

**Mango Coconut Sorbet:** Replace 2 tablespoons of lime juice with 3 tablespoons of full-fat coconut milk. The fat from the coconut milk adds richness and further inhibits large crystal formation.

**Mango Chili:** Add ½ teaspoon of ancho chili powder and a pinch of cayenne to the blend. The heat blooms beautifully against the cold, sweet mango.

**Mango Mint:** Add 10 fresh mint leaves to the blender in the first blend step. Strain through a fine mesh sieve before the first freeze to remove any fibrous mint bits.
